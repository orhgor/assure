Assure

What Assure is

A document verification tool.

You give it a source and a question. It writes the document — then checks every claim in that document against the source it came from. Each claim carries a verification state you can see at a glance.

Assure does not produce an ungrounded document. When claims cannot be traced directly to source evidence, they are isolated and flagged for review — and a document that fails grounding is refused outright.

What verification looks like

Three checks run — on claims, and on labelled figures.

Is there a source for this claim? The claim is matched to a specific sentence or passage in your uploaded material. If nothing matches, the claim is unanchored.

Does the source support this claim? The matched passage is read against the claim by a verification model. The verdict is supported, partial, or unsupported.

Do the numbers reconcile? A figure the draft states as a labelled measure is checked against the source, independently of the prose. A mismatch flags both values. A draft that states none reports a skipped check, not a pass.

The result is a distribution, not a yes/no. The counters in the right pane show it — how many claims anchored, supported, partial, unverified.

Assure treats your sources as untrusted data. Text inside an uploaded source that looks like an instruction is flagged and wrapped as content, not obeyed. If the compiled output drifts from the source — whether from a hallucination or from an instruction embedded in the material — the document is refused before you see it.

The adversarial layer

Red-Hat reads the verified draft the way a skeptical colleague would. It flags claims stated as fact that the source only implies, coverage language that shifts meaning between paragraphs, assertions about intent that nothing supports.

Findings quote the sentence they're worried about, and the source wording behind it. Each finding cites the paragraph it's about. You decide whether to apply the suggested fix.

Two defenses, layered. Verification asks: is this true to the source? Red-Hat asks: is this defensible in your hands?

What you'll find in the shell

Evidence — click any paragraph. The source, the page, and the quoted sentence the claim was drawn from.

Math Check — the numeral audit. Every labelled figure, its source line, its verdict; a draft that states none is reported as skipped.

Red-Hat — the adversarial review. Runs on any paragraph; returns specific findings with the source behind each.

Surgical Edit — click a paragraph or a Red-Hat finding. The rephrase editor opens, pre-filled from a finding. Every version is preserved.

Compare — two models on the same question, side by side. Read both, keep the one you prefer.

Search — across your uploaded sources. Click a result to open it in the editor.

Export — a compliance report: the compiled document, the numeral audit, every finding, the sign-offs and the document lock hash, with the truth ledger in the appendix. One file for a colleague or an auditor.

What's underneath

Your sources are stored in an open format — they move with you. The model that does the writing can be swapped without touching your data. Nothing here locks you in.

What the buyer should ask

Twelve questions. Each one has a specific answer the demo can show.

On accuracy

1. What happens when the AI is wrong?

The system does not produce a document it cannot ground. Claims that cannot be traced are flagged, not smoothed over. A document that fails grounding is refused outright. Nothing persists.

2. How do I know the model isn't hallucinating?

Every claim carries an explicit verification state. Anchored claims link directly to their source sentence and page. Any claim the model generated that cannot be grounded in the source is visually isolated and flagged as unanchored — never hidden in flowing prose. A document with no grounding at all is refused.

3. What about the numbers?

Every figure is checked against the source independently of the prose. The numeral audit runs separate from the language check. A mismatch flags both values side by side.

On control

4. Can I see what the model was asked and which model answered?

Yes. The left pane shows the compiled prompt and the routed model. Both are persisted across a reload.

5. What if I want a second opinion from another model?

Compare runs two models on the same question. Read both, keep the one you prefer. Per-clause merging across models is on the roadmap.

6. Can I edit a claim without breaking the rest of the document?

Yes. Surgical Edit operates on one paragraph at a time. The rest of the document is untouched. Every version is preserved and navigable.

On adversarial input

7. What if someone uploads a source with instructions embedded in it?

The source scan flags instruction-like content and wraps it as untrusted data. The pre-persist validator refuses any document whose output drifts from the source — whether the drift is a hallucination or an injected token.

8. Can the system be manipulated into revealing its prompt or producing arbitrary output?

Direct and indirect injection variants have been tested. Both are refused. The prompt is not disclosed. The document is not produced.

On audit and compliance

9. When the regulator asks how a decision was made, what do I show them?

The dossier: the compiled document, every source, every finding, the version history. Every claim is traceable to a source sentence and page. Every verification state is recorded.

10. Does the system support the NAIC bulletin requirements for explainability and auditability?

Every claim is anchored and traceable. Every version preserved. Every finding documented. The export produces a single audit artifact.

On vendor risk

11. What happens if you change the model, or the vendor relationship ends?

The model layer is swappable — it was changed without touching the data or the UI. Your sources are stored in an open format and move with you. Nothing locks you in.

12. What does the system not do?

It does not search the internet. Sources are what you upload — no open-web retrieval. It does not make legal or actuarial judgments; it reports what the source says and whether the claim matches. The underwriter decides. It does not replace human sign-off. And it does not guarantee 100% legal defensibility — it produces a document that is traceable, defensible, and auditable.

How Assure runs in the company

The full cycle, from submission to filed dossier.

Step 1 — The submission arrives

A commercial property renewal. The broker submits three documents: the expiring policy, the proposed renewal terms, and a cover note. The underwriter opens Assure, selects the account's project, and uploads all three. Each lands in the manifest — filename, size, indexed date. Search returns them. The source scan checks each for instruction-like content. None flagged.

Time: two minutes.

Step 2 — The underwriter asks

The question is what the file requires:

"Summarize the coverage limits, deductibles, and exclusions in the renewal. Flag anything that changed from the current policy. Cite each claim."

This is the same memo the underwriter would write. The difference is what happens next.

Step 3 — The compile

The pipeline runs — Retrieve, Draft, Anchor, Verify. The stages tick in the left pane. The progress line fills under the header. The underwriter waits while it runs. The left pane shows the compiled prompt that was sent to the model and the model that answered.

Step 4 — The document appears, verified

The memo renders in the center. Every paragraph carries a verification state: green underline (supported), amber underline (partially supported), grey (unanchored).

The counters report the distribution. The underwriter knows before reading a line which claims are clean, which need attention, and which are ungrounded.

If the compile produces zero anchors — a mismatched source, for instance — the document is refused outright. Nothing is persisted.

Step 5 — Reading with the evidence in reach

Any paragraph can be clicked. The right pane opens with the source filename, the page number, and the quoted sentence the claim was drawn from. Reading the memo is no longer an act of trust. It is an act of checking — one click per claim.

Step 6 — The numeral audit

Every figure in the memo is checked against the source independently of the prose — limits, deductibles, percentages, effective dates. A mismatch flags both values in the Math Check tab.

Step 7 — Red-Hat finds what a human would miss

The underwriter clicks the paragraph the counters flagged as partial. Red-Hat returns a finding:

"The memo states cyber liability is excluded. The source states cyber liability is excluded except for data breach response costs up to $250,000. The memo omits the exception. Suggested fix: add the carve-out."

The claim was 80% right. The 20% wrong would have mattered in a coverage dispute. The system caught it — and it quotes the sentence it's worried about and the source line behind it.

Step 8 — The underwriter applies the fix

Click Apply. The rephrase editor opens, pre-filled with the suggested fix. Submit. A new version lands. The document re-verifies. The counters update. The version navigator moves between revisions. Every state is preserved.

Step 9 — The reviewer signs off

The memo goes to the underwriting manager. She opens the same project. Everything is on screen: the document, the counters, the evidence, the Red-Hat finding, the version history. Her sign-off is not a rubber stamp. It is a review of a document whose every claim has already been checked.

Step 10 — The dossier is filed

The underwriter exports the dossier: the compiled memo, every source document, every finding, the version history. One file. It goes into the file.

Three years later, when a claim is disputed and the regulator asks how the renewal decision was made, the file contains the memo as it was written, the sources it was built from, every claim traceable to a specific page and sentence, every finding the adversarial review surfaced, every version of every edit.

The audit trail was produced by the system that made the document — not reconstructed after the fact.
